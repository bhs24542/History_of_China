HISTORY OF CHINA

PREFACE

Chinese civilization is the only great civilization in the world that has continued uninterruptedly and developed in the form of a state to this day. From the earliest spark of the Stone Age to the evolution of millennia of imperial rule, and onward to the transformation of modern and contemporary China, five thousand years of history have forged the profound and enduring heritage of Chinese civilization. Countless heroes and eminent figures have left their mark on this land, writing extraordinary chapters in the history of China.

EPILOGUE

As Chairman Mao Zedong wrote in his renowned poem Qinyuanchun · Snow:

“Our motherland so rich in beauty
Has made countless heroes vie to pay her their duty.
But alas! Qin Huang and Han Wu
In culture not well bred,
And Tang Zong and Song Zu
In letters not wide read.
And Genghis Khan, proud son of Heaven for a day,

They have all passed away;

This is not only the grandeur of history, but a clarion call of our era. The long river of history surges ever forward. For five thousand years, Chinese civilization has endured countless vicissitudes yet thrived through the ages, continuously renewed with vitality and vigor amid the transformations of each epoch.

Standing at a new historical starting point, hundreds of millions of Chinese people—armed with confidence, self-reliance, and strength—are forging ahead on the new journey and striving in the new era, marching courageously toward the grand goal of building a modern socialist country in all respects and realizing the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation, writing the heroic epic of our time.

Paleolithic Age

The Paleolithic Age in China extends from the earliest known human occupation to around 10,000 BC, encompassing hundreds of thousands of years of human development. Rather than a unified culture or political entity, it was a long and diverse period in which different groups of early humans lived across the vast landscapes of what is now China. They survived primarily through hunting, gathering, and fishing, gradually adapting to different environments while developing increasingly sophisticated stone tools, controlling fire, and forming early communities.

Archaeological discoveries at sites such as Zhoukoudian, Lantian, and Yuanmou provide important evidence of early human activity in China. Stone tools became progressively more varied and specialized, while evidence of controlled fire points to growing mastery of the natural environment. Over time, humans developed more organized patterns of habitation and cooperation, laying the technological and social foundations for later developments. By the end of the Paleolithic, increasingly complex tools, symbolic expression, and changing patterns of settlement foreshadowed the major transformations of the Neolithic Age.

Picture: Zhoukoudian Peking Man Site

Neolithic Age

The Neolithic Age in China, beginning around 10,000 BC and lasting until the emergence of early states around the 2nd millennium BC, was a period of profound transformation from mobile hunter-gatherer societies toward settled agricultural communities. Across the Yellow River, Yangtze River, and other regions, people increasingly cultivated crops, domesticated animals, built permanent settlements, and developed pottery and other specialized crafts. Although these communities remained culturally diverse, their growing populations, economic production, and social organization laid many of the foundations for later Chinese civilization.

The period saw the emergence of numerous regional cultures, including the Peiligang, Yangshao, Longshan, Hemudu, and Liangzhu cultures, each with distinctive traditions and forms of settlement. Painted pottery, polished stone tools, jade craftsmanship, rammed-earth architecture, and increasingly complex ceremonial practices reveal the growing sophistication of Neolithic societies. Large settlements and elaborate ritual objects also suggest increasing social differentiation and cooperation, while the development of agriculture and water management supported larger populations. By the late Neolithic, some communities had developed substantial centers of power and increasingly complex social structures, providing important archaeological foundations for the emergence of early states in ancient China.

Picture: Yangshao Painted Pottery Basin

Xia Dynasty

The Xia Dynasty (c. 2070–c. 1600 BC) is traditionally regarded as the first hereditary dynasty in Chinese history, marking the transition from earlier tribal alliances and chiefdoms toward a more established state society. Its historical core is generally associated with the Central Plains of northern China, particularly the Yellow River basin. Traditional accounts credit Yu the Great, who was said to have founded the dynasty after successfully controlling the great floods and passing political power to his son, establishing hereditary succession.

The Xia is remembered above all for the emergence of early state institutions, bronze production, large settlements, and increasingly complex social organization. Archaeological discoveries at sites such as Erlitou reveal palatial structures, bronze vessels, craft production, and signs of an emerging elite society dating to roughly this period. Traditional histories describe a line of hereditary rulers lasting until the reign of Jie, whose alleged misrule was followed by the rise of the Shang.

Picture: Erlitou Bronze Jue

✕

Shang Dynasty

The Shang Dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BC) was an early Bronze Age kingdom that emerged in the Yellow River basin and developed one of the most sophisticated bronze cultures of the ancient world. The Shang established several capitals during its history, with Yin at present-day Anyang becoming its final and most important center. Its political influence was concentrated in northern and central China, where powerful royal courts governed a network of settlements and regional territories through warfare, ritual, and kinship.

The Shang is especially known for its extraordinary bronze ritual vessels and oracle-bone inscriptions, which provide the earliest substantial body of Chinese writing known today. The royal court used divination to address questions of warfare, agriculture, weather, and state affairs, leaving records that reveal much about Shang society and beliefs. Bronze casting reached a remarkable level of sophistication, while ancestor worship and elaborate ritual traditions became central features of political life. In 1046 BC, the Shang was defeated by the Zhou, whose rulers presented the conquest as a transfer of the Mandate of Heaven.

Picture: Oracle Bone Inscription

Zhou Dynasty

The Zhou Dynasty (1046–256 BC) was the longest-lasting major dynasty in Chinese history and one of the most influential periods in the development of Chinese political and intellectual traditions. The Western Zhou established its political center around Haojing near present-day Xi'an, while the Eastern Zhou later moved the capital to Luoyang. Its realm was centered on the Yellow River basin and gradually extended across much of northern and central China, though political control varied considerably over time. The Zhou introduced the idea of the Mandate of Heaven, which became one of the foundational concepts of Chinese political thought.

The early Zhou maintained authority through a network of regional lords bound by kinship, ritual, and political obligations, but royal power weakened during the later centuries. The Eastern Zhou was divided into the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, when rival states competed for territory and political supremacy. At the same time, China experienced one of its greatest periods of intellectual development, producing Confucius, Mencius, Laozi, Mozi, and other major thinkers whose ideas shaped Chinese philosophy for centuries. Constant warfare also encouraged administrative, military, and technological innovations, ultimately creating the conditions for the Qin conquest and reunification of China.

Picture: Zenghouyi Bells

Qin Dynasty

The Qin Dynasty (221–207 BC) was the first unified multi-ethnic centralized state in Chinese history. It was established by Qin Shi Huang, the ruler of Qin who defeated the rival states of the Warring States period and proclaimed himself the First Emperor. The capital was Xianyang, near present-day Xi'an, and the Qin Empire was centered on the northern and central plains while extending into regions to the south and southwest. Despite its short existence, the Qin fundamentally transformed the political structure of China.

The Qin replaced the former system of competing feudal states with centrally appointed commanderies and counties, while standardizing writing, currency, weights and measures across the empire. Major infrastructure projects, including roads, defensive walls, and canals, strengthened imperial control and military mobility, while the immense Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang and the Terracotta Army became enduring symbols of the dynasty. Qin rule was also marked by harsh laws, heavy taxation, and large-scale labor demands, which contributed to widespread unrest after the emperor's death. Rebellion and civil war soon brought the dynasty to an end, paving the way for the Han.

Picture: Terracotta Warriors

✕

Han Dynasty

The Han Dynasty (202 BC–220 AD) was one of the foundational periods of imperial Chinese history, consolidating the centralized political system created under the Qin while establishing institutions that would influence China for centuries. Founded by Liu Bang, who became Emperor Gaozu after the collapse of the Qin, the dynasty initially ruled from Chang'an and later established its capital at Luoyang during the Eastern Han. Its territory centered on the Yellow River and Yangtze basins and expanded significantly into the northwest, south, and southwest, while Han influence also extended deep into Central Asia and toward the Korean Peninsula.

The early Han saw periods of relative stability such as the Rule of Wen and Jing, followed by the expansionist reign of Emperor Wu, under whom Han power reached far into Central Asia. The missions of Zhang Qian helped open new routes of communication that later became part of the Silk Road, linking China with Central and Western Asia. The Han period also witnessed major developments in Confucian state ideology, historical writing, science, technology, and the arts, while figures such as Sima Qian left enduring works of Chinese historiography. After the Wang Mang interregnum and the restoration of the dynasty as the Eastern Han, political instability, powerful court factions, and major rebellions eventually weakened the state, leading to its collapse in 220 AD and the beginning of the Three Kingdoms era.

Picture: Flying Horse of Gansu

Three Kingdoms

The Three Kingdoms period (220–280 AD) was an era of division following the collapse of the Eastern Han Dynasty, when China was dominated by the rival states of Wei, Shu, and Wu. Wei controlled much of northern China and was centered at Luoyang, Shu was based in the Sichuan Basin with Chengdu as its capital, and Wu ruled the lower Yangtze and southeastern regions from Jianye, present-day Nanjing. Although the period was relatively short, it became one of the most famous eras in Chinese history for its prolonged warfare, political rivalry, and military strategy.

The period began when Cao Pi established Wei in 220, followed by the establishment of Shu Han under Liu Bei and Wu under Sun Quan. The three states competed through major campaigns and shifting alliances, while figures such as Zhuge Liang, Sima Yi, and Zhou Yu became enduring symbols of strategy and political intrigue. Battles such as Guandu, Red Cliffs, and Yiling shaped the balance of power, while advances in administration, agriculture, and military organization supported the competing states. In 263, Wei conquered Shu, and in 265 the Sima clan established the Western Jin, which finally conquered Wu in 280 and reunited China.

Picture: Chengdu Wuhou Shrine

✕

Jin Dynasty

The Jin Dynasty (266–420 AD) was established by Sima Yan, who founded the Western Jin Dynasty and briefly reunified China in 280 after conquering Wu. With Luoyang as its main capital, the Western Jin ruled a territory broadly centered on the traditional heartlands of northern and central China. After the dynasty's collapse, the imperial court moved south and established the Eastern Jin at Jiankang, present-day Nanjing, which controlled much of southern China while northern China was divided among competing regimes. The Jin period thus connected a brief reunification with a much longer era of political division and southward migration.

The Western Jin was weakened by the War of the Eight Princes and invasions from northern peoples, leading to the loss of the north and the fall of Luoyang and Chang'an. The Eastern Jin preserved the imperial court in the south while powerful aristocratic families dominated politics, and large-scale migration helped transform the Yangtze region into a major center of population and culture. At the same time, Buddhism and Daoism continued to spread, while developments in calligraphy, philosophy, and the arts contributed to the cultural heritage of the Six Dynasties. The Jin ultimately gave way to the Northern and Southern Dynasties, leaving a lasting legacy of political fragmentation and cultural transformation.

Picture: Lantingji Xu

Southern and Northern Dynasties

The Northern and Southern Dynasties (420–589 AD) was a prolonged era of political division in which a succession of states ruled northern and southern China. In the south, the Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, and Chen successively ruled from Jiankang, while the north was dominated by states established by different peoples, most notably the Northern Wei, which eventually established Luoyang as its capital. Despite constant warfare and shifting borders, the period also witnessed major population movements, political restructuring, and the integration of diverse cultural traditions across northern and southern China.

The Northern Wei unified much of northern China in 439 and later undertook important reforms, including the equal-field system and the introduction of more centralized institutions. Under Emperor Xiaowen, the court moved its capital to Luoyang and promoted cultural and political assimilation, while Buddhist art flourished in sites such as the Yungang and Longmen Grottoes. In the south, Jiankang became an important center of literature, philosophy, and aristocratic culture, while migration from the north helped accelerate the economic and cultural development of the Yangtze region. The period finally ended in 589, when the Sui Dynasty conquered the Chen, reunifying China.

Picture: Yungang Grottoes

✕

Sui Dynasty

The Sui Dynasty (581–618 AD) was established by Yang Jian, Emperor Wen of Sui, who conquered the Southern Chen in 589 and reunified China after nearly three centuries of division. Its principal capital was Daxing, present-day Xi'an, while Luoyang became an important secondary capital under Emperor Yang. The Sui ruled a vast territory spanning the traditional heartlands of northern and southern China and restored a centralized imperial government, laying important institutional foundations for the Tang Dynasty that followed.

The Sui strengthened central administration, reorganized the political structure, and expanded the imperial examination system, while the construction and expansion of the Grand Canal linked the economic centers of the north and south. Emperor Yang also launched major military campaigns against Goguryeo and undertook ambitious construction projects, placing enormous pressure on the state. Widespread unrest and rebellions eventually brought the dynasty to an end in 618, but its reunification of China, administrative reforms, and infrastructure projects provided the foundation for the prosperity of the succeeding Tang Dynasty.

Picture: Grand Canal

✕

Tang Dynasty

The Tang Dynasty (618–907 AD) was founded by Li Yuan after he rose to power amid the upheavals at the end of the Sui Dynasty. With Chang'an as its capital, it was the first major unified dynasty to reunify China after the Sui. Its realm was centered on the Yellow River and Yangtze River basins, extending westward into Central Asia, northward into the steppe regions, eastward toward the Korean Peninsula, and southward to Lingnan and parts of present-day northern Vietnam. The Tang inherited and further developed the institutions established under the Sui, achieving remarkable advances in politics, economy, military power, and culture, and creating one of the most distinctive eras of openness and prosperity in Chinese history.

The early Tang saw the Reign of Zhenguan and the Kaiyuan Prosperity, periods of political stability, economic development, and great national strength. The An Lushan Rebellion marked a turning point, after which the dynasty gradually declined amid regional military governors, powerful eunuchs, and growing political fragmentation, eventually falling in 907. The High Tang was also the golden age of Classical Chinese poetry, with poets such as Li Bai, Du Fu, and Wang Wei producing numerous enduring masterpieces. Chang'an attracted merchants, envoys, and monks from Central Asia, West Asia, Japan, and the Korean Peninsula, while the Silk Road and maritime routes facilitated extensive exchanges of goods, religions, and cultures, creating the open and cosmopolitan character later associated with the Tang spirit.

Picture: Tang Sancai

Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms

The Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period (907–979 AD) was an era of political fragmentation that followed the collapse of the Tang Dynasty. In northern China, five short-lived dynasties—Later Liang, Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han, and Later Zhou—successively controlled the central plains, with capitals shifting mainly between Kaifeng and Luoyang. At the same time, a number of independent kingdoms emerged across the south and other regions, including Wu, Southern Tang, Min, Chu, Former Shu, Later Shu, Jingnan, and Northern Han. The period was characterized by rapid political change, regional military rule, and the coexistence of multiple competing states.

Despite its instability, the period also saw important economic and cultural developments, particularly in the more prosperous south, where agriculture, commerce, and regional states continued to grow. The southern kingdoms helped lay the foundations for the later economic rise of the Yangtze basin, while developments in printing, painting, ceramics, and Buddhist art continued from the Tang tradition. The Later Zhou gradually strengthened central authority in the north, and in 960 Zhao Kuangyin founded the Song Dynasty. Over the following two decades, the Song conquered the remaining major southern states, bringing the fragmented era to an end in 979.

Picture: The Night Revels of Han Xizai

Song Dynasty

The Song Dynasty (960–1279 AD) was founded by Zhao Kuangyin following the Chenqiao Mutiny. Its capital was initially Kaifeng, before the Southern Song established its capital at Lin'an. It was a dynasty marked by extraordinary economic, cultural, and technological development. The Northern Song was centered on the Central Plains and the Yangtze River basin, extending southward to Lingnan and the coastal regions, while the Southern Song controlled mainly the territories south of the Yangtze as northern China remained divided among the Liao, Western Xia, Jin, and other regimes. Although the Song faced sustained military pressure, it achieved remarkable advances in institutions, economic development, and urban civilization.

The Song Dynasty consisted of the Northern Song and Southern Song periods. In the late Northern Song, the Jingkang Incident led to the fall of the northern capital and the Song court's retreat south, where the Southern Song was established. Meanwhile, the economic center of China continued to shift southward, while urban commerce and overseas trade flourished, and technologies such as movable-type printing, gunpowder, and the compass continued to develop. Ci poetry became one of the most distinctive literary forms of the period, while Neo-Confucianism, landscape painting, ceramics, and calligraphy reached new heights. Cities such as Kaifeng and Lin'an displayed highly developed commercial life and urban culture, creating a distinctive Song civilization.

Picture: Along the River During the Qingming Festival

Yuan Dynasty

The Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368 AD) was founded by Kublai, the Mongol ruler who established his capital at Dadu. In 1279, the Yuan conquered the Southern Song and completed the unification of China, becoming the first major unified dynasty in Chinese history established and ruled by a northern non-Han people. Its vast realm stretched from the northeast and the Korean region in the east, through Yunnan and Lingnan in the south, to the broad grasslands and regions of the northwest and the Western Regions. It formed an important political space spanning much of Eurasia. The Yuan inherited the governing institutions of previous Chinese dynasties while retaining elements of Mongol political traditions, creating a multi-ethnic imperial order.

During the Yuan Dynasty, the transportation and postal-station networks developed under the Mongol Empire strengthened connections across Eurasia. The overland Silk Road and maritime trade both flourished, bringing merchants, envoys, and travelers from Central Asia, West Asia, and even Europe into frequent contact with China. Travelers such as Marco Polo left accounts of Yuan China. At the same time, Yuan drama became an important literary form, while astronomy, medicine, and calendrical science made significant advances. Guo Shoujing played a leading role in compiling the Shoushi Calendar and advancing scientific and technological knowledge during the Yuan period.

Picture: Yuan Blue and White Porcelain

Ming Dynasty

The Ming Dynasty (1368–1644 AD) was founded by Zhu Yuanzhang after rising to power during the rebellions at the end of the Yuan Dynasty. Its capital was initially Nanjing, before the capital was moved to Beijing during the Yongle period. It was a major unified dynasty established by the Han Chinese following the Yuan. Its realm was centered on the Central Plains and the Yangtze River basin, with the Great Wall serving as an important defensive frontier in the north, while various levels of military and administrative institutions were established across the northeast, northwest, southwest, and southeastern coastal regions. The Ming left a distinctive mark on centralized government, urban development, overseas exchange, and the arts and culture.

The early Ming saw the Hongwu era and the Yongle period, during which centralization was further strengthened. The seven voyages of Zheng He promoted extensive exchanges between the Ming court and regions across South and Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean, while Beijing and the Forbidden City took shape during this period. Commercial activity and handicraft production continued to expand, while novels, drama, painting, and calligraphy flourished. Literary works such as Romance of the Three Kingdoms, Journey to the West, Water Margin, and The Plum in the Golden Vase became widely known. In the late Ming, however, the dynasty faced growing financial, military, and political pressures and eventually collapsed amid mounting internal and external challenges.

Picture: Forbidden City

✕

Qing Dynasty

The Qing Dynasty (1644–1912 AD) was established by the Manchu ruling house. Nurhaci laid the foundations of the Later Jin state, while Hong Taiji adopted the name "Great Qing" in 1636. After entering China proper, the Qing established Beijing as its capital and founded the last imperial dynasty in Chinese history. Its realm was centered on the traditional heartland of China and the eighteen provinces of the inner territories, while Qing rule was gradually extended and consolidated over Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet, and Taiwan, creating one of the largest territorial realms of any imperial dynasty in Chinese history. During the early and middle Qing, political order was relatively stable, while the population, agriculture, and handicraft industries continued to grow.

The early Qing experienced the expansion and consolidation of the Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong reigns, during which central authority was further strengthened and the empire's territorial boundaries were gradually consolidated. At the same time, population growth, commercial development, and expanding cities contributed to social prosperity, while novels, drama, painting, and the evidential scholarship tradition flourished. From the late eighteenth century onward, however, the Qing faced increasingly severe internal and external pressures. The First Opium War and the Taiping Rebellion, among other events, severely disrupted the traditional order. Although the Self-Strengthening Movement and other late Qing reforms sought to respond to the crisis, they ultimately failed to prevent the end of imperial rule. In 1912, the abdication of the Qing emperor brought more than two thousand years of imperial monarchy in China to an end.

Picture: Summer Palace

Republic of China

The Republic of China (1912–1949) was established after the Xinhai Revolution overthrew the Qing government. On January 1, 1912, Sun Yat-sen assumed office as the Provisional President in Nanjing, bringing an end to more than two thousand years of imperial rule in China and establishing the first bourgeois democratic republic in Asia. However, the achievements of the Xinhai Revolution were soon usurped by Yuan Shikai, and China subsequently became increasingly subject to the rule of the Beiyang warlords and prolonged warlord conflicts, leaving the country politically unstable and society deeply divided. The semi-colonial and semi-feudal nature of Chinese society remained unchanged, while the national crisis deepened, and the Chinese people continued to explore ways to save the nation and seek a path to national survival amid immense suffering.

The May Fourth Movement of 1919 promoted the spread of Marxism in China, and the founding of the Communist Party of China in 1921 brought about a profound change in the course of the Chinese revolution. In 1924, the First KMT-CPC Cooperation was established, and the Northern Expedition dealt a heavy blow to the rule of the Beiyang warlords. In 1931, Japan occupied Northeast China, and in 1937 the full-scale War of Resistance Against Japanese Aggression broke out. Under the banner of the United Front for Resistance Against Japanese Aggression, the Communist Party of China played the role of the mainstay of the nation's resistance, leading the people in a war of national resistance and opening up the enemy's rear areas, making an important contribution to victory in the War of Resistance Against Japanese Aggression. In 1945, the Chinese people achieved victory in the War of Resistance Against Japanese Aggression. After the war, the Kuomintang reactionaries launched a full-scale civil war, and the Communist Party of China led the people to victory in the War of Liberation. In April 1949, the People's Liberation Army launched the Crossing-the-Yangtze Campaign and liberated Nanjing, marking the overthrow of the Kuomintang's reactionary rule on the Chinese mainland. On October 1 of the same year, the People's Republic of China was founded, marking the great victory of China's New-Democratic Revolution, and the historical period of the Republic of China on the Chinese mainland thus came to an end.

Picture: Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum

People's Republic of China

The People’s Republic of China was founded on October 1, 1949, with Beijing as its capital. It is a socialist country under the people’s democratic dictatorship. Its founding brought an end to the history of China as a semi-colonial and semi-feudal society in modern times, achieved national independence and the liberation of the people, and opened a new chapter in Chinese history. Since its founding, China has continuously explored the path of socialism, and profound changes have taken place in the country’s overall development, the living standards of its people, and its international standing, gradually moving from standing up and growing prosperous to becoming strong. The development of the People’s Republic of China is a history of the Chinese people striving arduously and working hard to build a strong nation, as well as an important stage in the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation.

In the early years after the founding of New China, faced with a situation in which everything was waiting to be rebuilt, the country carried out socialist transformation, established the basic socialist system, and launched large-scale socialist construction, gradually establishing an independent industrial system and national economic system and laying a solid foundation for subsequent development. Following the Third Plenary Session of the 11th CPC Central Committee in 1978, China carried out reform and opening up, continuously deepened economic structural reform, expanded opening up to the outside world, and gradually established a socialist market economy. China’s economy developed rapidly and continuously, its overall national strength increased significantly, and it became the world’s second-largest economy. The living standards of the people progressed from inadequate food and clothing to moderate prosperity overall, while infrastructure development, science and technology, education, culture, health, and other undertakings made remarkable progress. Hong Kong and Macao returned to the motherland, and China made great strides to catch up with the times, achieving a great transformation from standing up to growing prosperous.

Since the 18th National Congress of the Communist Party of China, socialism with Chinese characteristics has entered a new era. The CPC Central Committee with Comrade Xi Jinping at its core has united and led the entire Party and the people of all ethnic groups across the country, giving shape to and establishing Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era. China has secured a moderately prosperous society in all respects, historically solved the problem of absolute poverty, and achieved the First Centenary Goal. The cause of the Party and the country has made historic achievements and undergone historic changes. The Chinese path to modernization continues to advance, and the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation has entered an irreversible historical process. Today, the Chinese people are advancing with confidence in the great cause of building a strong country and realizing national rejuvenation. From standing up and growing prosperous to becoming strong, China stands with an increasingly vigorous and confident posture among the nations of the world.
